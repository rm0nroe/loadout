# Article card (Sepia rules)

Adapted from Sepia's technical-articles domain (see `../PROVENANCE.md`). Read `professional.md` first, including its outline check. Covers engineering posts, tutorials, architecture write-ups, essays and experience reports. It writes text only; the user publishes it. A long or externally published article is consequential: use independent review (`review.md`).

## Baseline

Motivated by a real problem the author actually hit. Uneven by design: deep where it got interesting, one line where it did not. It has an opinion a reader could disagree with and numbers with their conditions attached. First person and contractions are normal.

## Gather facts (read-only)

The user's notes, the incident or work that prompted it, real benchmarks with machine, version, dataset and run count, code that was actually run, the sources to cite. If there is no real situation, write "notes on X"; never fabricate an incident.

## Rules

1. The problem before the topic. The first paragraph is the concrete situation that forced the question, not a survey of the field or a definition the reader already knows.
2. At least one opinion, stated as the author's, with the condition under which it stops applying ("if your writes are under 1k/s, ignore this").
3. Depth by interest, not symmetry. The section that surprised the author gets several times the words of the setup. Every alternative getting a paragraph and none getting a verdict is a defect; commit to a recommendation.
4. Include what broke and what was tried first, only when the user supplied it. Never invent a dead end.
5. Numbers keep the precision the notes give (never round 212.4 to 212) and carry their conditions, or are not printed. Never add a before/after figure, a comparison or a causal explanation the notes do not state. Claims carry links. Code is real and run, or is marked as a sketch. No invented `foo` examples presented as tested.
6. No listicle in prose clothing ("The first... The second..."): use an honest list or table, or real prose with an argument. No coined concept labels; use plain description or an established term. No fractal summaries.
7. Quoted material and cited sources stay exact, with the URL. Never cite an unread source.
8. End on the recommendation or the open question the author actually has. No both-sides conclusion or future outlook.
9. Voice: first person, contractions, an aside or two, as the author's profile and past posts show. Never forced casualness.
