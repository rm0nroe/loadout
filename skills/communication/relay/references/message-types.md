# Message types

The spine from SKILL.md holds across all of these: header with the ask in the Re line, boundary first, evidence throughout, actions grouped by when, one concrete action at the close. What changes is which middle sections earn their space and where the reader's resistance sits.

Diagnose the type by asking what the reader's first reaction will be. That reaction is what the message has to absorb.

---

## Review or critique feedback

Reader's first reaction: defensiveness. They made these choices for reasons.

- Credit is not optional here. It is the section that buys you the right to be critical, and it has to be specific enough that only someone who read the work could have written it.
- Attribute problems to the artifact, never the author. "`plan:2025` passes the wrong term" not "you passed the wrong term."
- Where the reader already agreed with a principle and simply failed to apply it, say so. "This contradicts your own constraint at line 34" ends the argument before it starts.
- Severity tiers help the reader triage, but only if you are honest about them. Inflating a nit to a blocker costs you the next blocker.
- If a prior round of feedback exists, a closure table (closed / partial / open) is usually the reader's most-wanted section, because their real question is which of their fixes landed.

## Status update

Reader's first reaction: is this on track, and do I need to do anything.

- Lead with the state, not the activity. "Three of eight done, one blocker, no help needed" beats a chronology.
- Say explicitly when no action is required. Readers assume a status message contains a hidden ask and will hunt for it.
- Distinguish slipped from at risk from blocked. These have different responses and readers conflate them.
- Credit and severity tiers usually do not apply. Keep it short; a status update that runs long implies problems you have not named.

## Ask or request

Reader's first reaction: what is this going to cost me.

- Cost up front: time, scope, deadline. Burying it reads as a trick and poisons the next ask.
- Say what you already tried and why it was not enough, or the reader's first reply is a suggestion you have already ruled out.
- Make the decline path explicit and safe. "If this is not your team, tell me who owns it" gets a fast useful answer instead of silence.
- Name a date and say what happens if it slips.

## Decision announcement

Reader's first reaction: was my input considered, and can I still change this.

- State the decision, who made it, and when, in the first two lines. Ambiguity about authority guarantees relitigation.
- Name the rejected alternatives. Readers who advocated for one need to see it was considered, not overlooked, and this is what stops the thread from reopening.
- Be exact about what is now closed and what remains open. Readers push on whatever you leave fuzzy.
- Give the reversal condition if there is one. "We revisit if the device spike fails" converts opposition into patience.

## Bad news, incident, or failure

Reader's first reaction: how bad, and what now.

- Impact first, in concrete terms. Who is affected, since when, what is broken.
- Facts before analysis, clearly separated. Confirmed findings and current hypotheses need different phrasings; readers act on both.
- No speculation about blame. It is wrong often enough to be dangerous and it derails the response every time.
- State what is already done, what is in flight, and when the next update lands. A committed next-update time is what stops the follow-up questions.
- Root cause can come later. Say so explicitly rather than leaving a gap.

## Escalation

Reader's first reaction: why is this mine.

- Open with what you need from this specific person, and why it can only come from them.
- Show the attempts already made, with dates. Escalation without that history reads as skipping the queue.
- Give the cost of continued inaction in their terms, not yours.
- Keep it short. Escalations are read by busy people and length reduces the chance of a decision.

## Handoff

Reader's first reaction: what am I inheriting and what is going to bite me.

- Current state first, then where everything lives, then the known traps.
- Be candid about what is unfinished, fragile, or undocumented. The receiver will find it anyway, and finding it unannounced destroys trust in the rest of the handoff.
- Name the decisions already made and locked, so they do not spend their first week reopening them.
- Say who to ask and for how long you remain available.
